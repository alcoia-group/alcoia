On the measurement of reading

The measurement of reading comprehension has a long and somewhat troubled history, beginning with the assumption that the movement of the eyes across a page constitutes a reliable proxy for the movement of attention through a text. This assumption is intuitive and largely wrong. A reader may fixate steadily on a paragraph while thinking about something else entirely, and may skip across a line while assembling its meaning perfectly well from partial information. The relationship between where the eyes point and what the mind does is real but weak, and it becomes weaker as the measurement apparatus becomes cheaper and noisier than the laboratory equipment on which the original findings were established.

Consider the difference between a laboratory eye tracker sampling at one thousand hertz with sub-degree spatial accuracy and a consumer webcam estimating gaze from a low-resolution image of a face under uncontrolled lighting. The former can distinguish individual fixations within a word; the latter can, at best, indicate which region of the screen a reader is facing. Treating the output of the second as though it carried the semantics established with the first is a category error, and one that is unfortunately easy to make because both produce a stream of coordinates that look superficially alike when plotted.

There is a more reliable class of signal available to any system running inside a browser, and it requires no camera at all. The rate at which a reader progresses through text, compared against the difficulty of that text and against the reader's own established pace, is a precise and continuously available measurement. So is the act of scrolling backwards to revisit a passage, which is an unambiguous behavioural marker of something having failed to land. These signals are not proxies for attention; they are direct observations of reading behaviour, and they cost nothing in permissions or privacy.

The objection to relying on such signals is that they are coarse, and this objection is correct but not decisive. A coarse measurement that is actually measuring the thing of interest is more useful than a fine measurement of something adjacent to it. The relevant question is not which sensor produces more data, but which sensor produces data that survives contact with the phenomenon being studied. In the case of reading, the answer has been available for some time and is largely ignored because eye tracking is more impressive to demonstrate.

A system that intervenes when it believes a reader is struggling inherits a further obligation, which is to be right often enough that the intervention is welcome rather than intrusive. Attention is the scarcest resource in the interaction, and every unnecessary interruption spends it. This produces an asymmetry that should govern the design of the entire system: a missed opportunity to help costs very little, while an unwanted interruption costs a great deal, and the correct response to uncertainty is therefore to do nothing at all.

The final consideration concerns what a system of this kind should say when it does decide to speak. An intervention that asserts an internal state to the reader is making a claim it cannot support, and readers are quick to notice when a machine claims to know their mind. An intervention that reports an observation is on much firmer ground. Telling somebody that they slowed down considerably on a particular passage is a statement about the record, and the reader is free to disagree with the inference drawn from it.
